The product this lives inside, in one slide.

The company

Songfinch sold one thing.

An original song written for you by a real artist. Around $200, about a week. It converted under two percent.

The product

Instant Song assembles instead of generating.

Artists record the lines that never change, plus every version of the lines that do. A customer's answers select which pieces play. Seconds, not a week.

The constraint

We were not allowed to generate songs.

The promise is that a real artist wrote it. Generation kills the promise and the artist network walks. That constraint is why the rest of this is interesting.

The outcome

It worked.

Roughly 4x the conversion of the product beside it at launch, and 150,000+ units over the two years that followed.

That is all the context you need. Everything from here is about one feature inside it: getting the song to say the recipient's name. It was the hardest thing we built and the only part customers reliably cried at.
01 · The problemWhy we started

Nobody cried at the personalized lyric. They cried at their name.

Instant Song already swapped lines based on customer answers. "You're always the life of the party." Nice, on-brand, and customers described it as clever.

Clever is not the reaction we were selling. The reaction we were selling happens about four seconds in, when a stranger's voice sings your name back at you.

So the question became: can the song say any name, in the artist's own voice, without us recording every artist saying 2,000 names?

Why it could not be brute-forced

2,000 names × every artist is not a recording session. It is a career.

The name list came from a 2,000-name catalogue. Multiply by the artists in round one, by the syllable and cadence variants each name needs, and you are asking working musicians for tens of thousands of takes.

No artist agrees to that, and if they did, the audio would be inconsistent across sessions. The brute-force version of this product does not exist at any budget.

01 · The problemThe reframe the whole build sits on

A name is not a string. It is a rhythm.

Text has no sound

Spelling does not tell you how a name is said.

"Sean" is one syllable. "Siobhan" is two. "Nguyen" is one. Letter-to-sound rules get all three wrong, and a name pronounced wrong is worse than no name at all.

Sung is not spoken

In singing, vowels carry the note.

Consonants are transients. Vowels hold pitch and duration. So a name's vowel nuclei are what have to land on beats, and that is a different problem from pronouncing it.

The slot is fixed

The melody has a budget.

The name sits in one line of the chorus with a set number of beats. One syllable and three syllables cannot occupy that budget the same way. The music has to bend, or the name breaks.

Once we framed it that way, the work stopped being "make a voice say a name" and became "map an arbitrary name onto a fixed musical slot, in a specific person's voice, and prove it before it ships." Four separate engineering problems, only one of which is machine learning.

02 · The inheritanceWhere the project was when we got it

The research had existed for years. The product had not.

A machine learning specialist had been working this problem inside the company for a long time before our team touched it. The hard science was real and it was good: the voice synthesis worked, the approach was sound.

What did not exist was everything around it. No dictionary anyone could trust at scale. No way for an artist to contribute their voice without a researcher sitting next to them. No QA gate. No throughput. No path from "this works in a session" to "this runs when a stranger types a name at 2am."

The music and innovation team picked it up and had it live within a year.

What that says about the job

Research to product is a different discipline, not a later phase of the same one.

Nothing was wrong with the work we inherited. It was solving the modelling problem, and the modelling problem was genuinely solved.

Shipping it required a different set of skills: artist operations, naming conventions, QA design, batch processing, and a compensation conversation. None of those are machine learning, and all of them were the reason it had not shipped.

This is the part of the story I would want to be asked about, because it is the closest thing I have done to the job being described.

Layer 4 is the machine learning. Layers 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 are operations, and every one of them was a reason the feature had not shipped before. That ratio is the whole reason I am interested in enablement work rather than modelling work.
03 · Layer 1The dictionary

You cannot generate a pronunciation dictionary for names.

Grapheme-to-phoneme conversion works acceptably on common English words because English spelling is irregular but statistically learnable. Names are the exact population where that breaks: they come from every language, they are deliberately variant-spelled, and two people spelling their name identically may say it differently.

So the dictionary was built and verified rather than predicted. Modified Arpabet, extended to cover sounds the standard set does not carry cleanly, and adjusted for sung rather than spoken articulation.

The work, honestly described

We listened to a lot of names.

Collect the list. Decide which names are in scope for round one. Listen to each one. Write the phoneme string. Have someone else check it. Flag the ones with more than one legitimate pronunciation, because those need a product decision, not a phonetic one.

The decision that saved us

Ambiguous names get excluded, not guessed.

If a name has two common pronunciations and we cannot tell which one a customer means, the correct product behaviour is to fall back to the general line, not to guess. A wrong name is worse than no name. That rule made the QA gate binary and made the whole thing shippable.

03 · Layer 3The capture instrument

We needed phonetic coverage from people who hate phonetics.

To synthesize any name in an artist's voice, the model needs that artist producing every vowel and consonant sound, across registers, with real performance data attached. Full coverage, not a nice sample.

The naive version of that ask is a phonetically-balanced script, which is a document that makes a working musician want to leave. So we did not send a script. We sent a game.

Internally we called it the Sonic Olympics. Three rounds, in this order, because the order matters as much as the content.

Round 1

Wild Warm Ups

Loosen the voice and get them past self-consciousness. Nothing here is precious, and that is the point.

Round 2

Meticulous Melodies

The controlled material. Sustained vowels, clean articulation, consistent pitch. This is where the usable vowel data comes from.

Round 3

Freaky Phrases

Deliberately awkward consonant clusters and sound combinations that never appear in a normal lyric, but do appear in names.

The template is the product. An artist could sit down with it, record their session unsupervised, and send back something the pipeline could actually use. That is the difference between a research technique that needs its inventor in the room and a system that scales across a roster.
03 · Layer 2Where the name goes

Every song got a blueprint down to the syllable.

For each Instant Song we mapped the exact line the name lands in, usually the first line of the chorus, and documented the slot: how many beats it occupies, how many syllables it can absorb, where the stress has to fall for the phrase to still sound like music.

Then the artist tracked that same line roughly sixteen times, covering the range of syllable counts and cadences a name might need.

Why sixteen takes and not one

The synth should place a name, not stretch one.

If you have one recording of the line and force every name into it, the model has to time-stretch, and stretching is exactly where synthetic voices announce themselves. A four-syllable name crammed into a one-syllable pocket sounds like software.

With sixteen real human performances of the same line, a name gets dropped into a pocket a person actually sang at that length. The realism came from the recording plan, not from the model.

03 · Layers 4 and 5Making it sound like them

The trick is that most of what you hear was never synthesized.

Layer 4 · Synthesis

Synthesizer V renders the name onto the melody.

Phonemes from the dictionary, note and duration from the blueprint, timbre from the artist's captured voice model. The output is the name, sung, in something close to that artist's voice.

Close is not good enough on its own. In isolation a synthesized name is convincing. Dropped into a real recording it is immediately audible, because the surrounding audio is a real human in a real room.

Layer 5 · Blend

The name is masked into the artist's real line.

The rest of the line is the artist's actual recorded performance. Only the name is synthetic, and it is blended into the take so the seam is not perceptible: matched to the room, the breath, the processing chain, and the take's own dynamics.

A listener hears an artist singing a line. The synthetic portion is a fraction of a second inside it. That is why it sounds like them, and it is also why it sits comfortably inside a promise that a real artist made this.

This is the architectural decision I am proudest of. The minimum viable amount of the output is synthetic, which simultaneously solved the realism problem, the cost problem, and the ethics problem. The artist is still the performer. We are inserting a word, not manufacturing a singer.
03 · Layer 6The unglamorous gate

Every name, every artist, listened to by a person.

There is no automated metric for "does this sound like a mistake." Formant distance and spectral similarity tell you the synthesis matched the model. They do not tell you the result is embarrassing, and embarrassing is the failure mode that costs a customer.

So the gate was human and binary: pass, or fail with a reason. Failures went to one of three buckets: wrong pronunciation back to the dictionary, wrong pocket back to the blueprint, wrong voice back to the model.

Routing a failure to the right layer is most of what made the loop converge instead of thrash.

The number that made it finite

Names are Zipf-distributed, and that is the whole reason this shipped.

A small fraction of first names covers a large majority of real customers. So the work was never "2,000 names perfect before launch." It was: rank by real frequency, gate the top of that list to a high standard, and fall back gracefully on the long tail.

Coverage of customers, not coverage of the dictionary. Same insight as prioritizing any AI rollout: find the head of the distribution and make that part excellent.

03 · Making it a productSpeed and engineering

A feature that takes a person a day is a demo, not a product.

Refining the audio processing

Early on, producing one artist's name set was a manual pass: render, import, align, match levels, apply the chain, bounce, name the file, check it. Multiply by names and by artists and the arithmetic kills you before the model ever does.

Most of my time went into collapsing that: batch rendering, a fixed processing chain applied programmatically instead of by hand, enforced naming so nothing needed a human to identify it, and FFmpeg pipelines doing the cleanup and versioning across thousands of assets. The same batch discipline the wider Instant Song build already ran on.

Working with engineering

The music and innovation team owned the pipeline that produced the assets. Engineering owned what happened when a customer typed a name at 2am: lookup, selection, assembly, delivery.

The interface between us was the thing that mattered, and it was mostly a naming contract. Every asset self-described which artist, which song, which line, which slot and which name it satisfied, so the backend could select without anyone interpreting anything.

The fallback logic was negotiated in the same conversation: what the product does when a name is not in the dictionary, which is play the general line and say nothing about it.

Nobody outside the team ever saw any of this layer, and it is the layer that turned a working technique into a shipping feature. It is also the work I am most confident transfers, because every company has a version of it and almost nobody staffs it deliberately.
03 · The hardest layerConsent, training, trust

We asked musicians for their voice data in the worst year to ask.

2023 and 2024 were peak AI panic in the music industry. The ask on paper was: let us capture your vowels, consonants and performance characteristics so software can produce sounds you did not personally sing.

That sentence ends most conversations, and it should. If the supply side of a marketplace decides you are a threat, you do not have a product, you have a lawsuit and an empty roster.

What actually worked
  • Scope stated precisely. Their voice model produces names, inside their own songs, on this product. Not a general-purpose voice. Not licensed out.
  • Pay attached to the ask. A guarantee for the session plus a royalty on songs sold. It is more work than a normal song and the compensation said so.
  • The template did the training. Artists did not need to understand phonemes. They needed a session template and three rounds of instructions they could follow alone.
  • Opt-in, per artist, round by round. We started with a small group who wanted in, and let it spread on their word rather than ours.

Every technical decision upstream was constrained by whether this conversation would go well. That is not a values slide. It determined the architecture.

04 · OutcomeThe honest part

Three things I got wrong, and one I would do differently on day one.

01

We scoped round one to romantic relationships.

Which meant the wider product's category list did not fit real customers, and people bounced off the form trying to describe a friend, a parent, a kid. Starting with the narrowest emotional case made the engineering cleaner and the product worse.

02

QA effort was estimated per name, not per artist.

The same name in a new voice is a new asset needing a new listen. Effort scaled with names multiplied by artists, not added to them, and we felt that late.

03

Ambiguous pronunciations arrived as a product question dressed as a phonetics question.

We treated it as a dictionary problem for too long before deciding the answer was a fallback rule.

The day-one fix, and it is the same one from the wider launch: instrument first. We could not easily answer which names were actually being requested, which were falling back, or which artists had the weakest coverage. All three were sitting in logs nobody was reading, and each one would have redirected the QA effort weeks earlier.
04 · CloseWhy I told you this one

The model was never the bottleneck.

It had already worked for years when we picked it up. What was missing was every operational layer around it, and building those is the job I am describing to you.

Transfers 01

Find the layer that is actually blocking.

It is rarely the model. Here it was a dictionary, a recording protocol, a QA gate and a naming contract.

Transfers 02

Make the human contribution unsupervised.

A technique that needs its inventor in the room does not scale. A template that a musician can run alone does.

Transfers 03

Head of the distribution first.

Cover the customers, not the catalogue, and fall back gracefully on the tail.

Transfers 04

The supply side has a veto.

In any marketplace, the people supplying the value can walk. Scope, pay and honesty upstream of the build, or there is no build.
