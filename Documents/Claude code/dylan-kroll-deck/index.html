00The idea everything else hangs on

A song is not atomic.

In 2023 I helped build a product that hands a customer a personalized song in about eight seconds. Not by generating it. By assembling it.

Artists recorded the parts that never change and, separately, every version of the parts that do. We called the variable parts stems. A customer's answers select which stems play. Human-made, machine-assembled, instant.

The reason that worked is the reason most AI rollouts fail. The team wasn't asking for a whole song.

The move

Decompose the job before you automate it.

Find the smallest unit that is reusable and checkable. Hand the machine that. Keep the human in the seat where judgment actually lives.

Why it transfers

Every failed rollout I have seen asked for a song.

"Write our proposals." "Handle onboarding." Too big to verify, too vague to trust, too far from the work to survive a week.

Scenario 01Discovery

"Claude isn't helping my team."

First: I do not defend the tool and I do not offer training. Both are the same mistake, which is treating a symptom as the problem. A VP saying this is handing me a free diagnostic. I ask five questions.

  1. Walk me through the last time someone tried and it didn't work.
    A specific incident beats a general complaint. General complaints have no failure point in them.
  2. What did they expect to get back?
    This is where I find out whether they asked for a whole song.
  3. What do they do on the days they don't use it?
    That is the real workflow, and the baseline I will measure against.
  1. Which part of this would you not trust a machine with?
    Finds the trust boundary. Human review belongs there and nowhere else.
  2. If it worked perfectly, what would your team stop doing?
    The outcome, in their words and their numbers. Not mine.
What I commit to on that call: a baseline agreed before I build anything. If we cannot name the number today, we are not ready to build tomorrow.
Scenario 01 · contDiagnosis · Measurable outcomes

It is almost always one of four.

01

Wrong altitude

The ask is a whole song. Nobody can verify a whole song, so nobody trusts it.

02

No context

The model has never seen the brand book, the past campaigns, or the account history. It is guessing, competently.

03

No verdict

Output arrives with no way to tell good from bad, so checking costs more than doing.

04

Wrong seat

The person holding the tool is not the person who feels the pain.

Then: one recurring task, decomposed, with the two most boring and most checkable stems handed back inside the tool they already use, by the end of the week. Not a training session. A shipped stem.

Scenario 02 · contThe part that decides whether I get a second sprint

Saying no is half the job.

A silent "later" is how enablement loses the room. Nobody gets a no. Everybody gets a date and a reason.

Finance · this sprint

Invoice categorization ships.

Objectively checkable against a ledger, zero adoption resistance, one sprint to a defensible number. That number funds everything after it.

Sales · one day of my sprint

Instrumented, not built.

I spend a day measuring where proposal time actually goes. When Sales gets its sprint, it gets built against a baseline instead of a guess. Highest value deserves the best-prepared build.

Customer Success · next sprint, in writing

A date and the reason.

Onboarding is high value and high adoption risk, because the CS voice is the product. It needs the trust that sprint one buys.

If the VP of Sales pulls rank

That is a real dynamic and I do not fight it. There is a zero-cost version of proposal automation: the current best proposal, the brand context, and a shared prompt, shipped in an afternoon. It is not the build. It is discovery that happens to be useful, and its usage data tells me what the real build should be.

What I am actually optimizing in sprint one

Not ROI. Evidence. Sprint one buys the credibility that makes sprints two through ten possible. Picking the highest-value request first and shipping something unverifiable is the most common way this role stalls out in its first quarter.

Scenario 03The adoption cliff

They tried it once. That is good news.

Rejection is never trying. One try means the pitch worked and the product didn't, so something specific happened on that first run. There is a failure point and it has a location.

Most likely

It broke on a real input

It worked on the demo case. Theirs had a 40-page PDF or an apostrophe in a client name. Nobody files a bug for an internal tool. They just stop.

Very likely

It lives in the wrong place

I built a tool. Their work happens in Slack, the CRM, a spreadsheet. Every context switch is a tax, and the tax exceeded the saving.

Common

Checking cost more than doing

They cannot tell if the output is right without redoing it. So they redo it.

Quietly common

I saved the wrong 20 minutes

I automated the part that was fun to build, not the part that was slow and hated.

How I find out

  • Not a survey. People cannot tell you why they stopped. They will say they have been busy.
  • The logs. Where did the last session end? That step is the bug.
  • Watch three people do it the old way. Forty-five minutes, and I will see the workaround they never mentioned.

Then I rebuild smaller

  • One stem, inside the surface they already have open, with verification in the output.
  • Three people, not thirty. Adoption is earned per person before it is announced.
  • And I say the true sentence: I built the wrong thing, here is the smaller thing. That buys more credibility than a working v1.

Deep dive · 01The problem

The funnel had a wall, and the obvious fix was the wrong one.

Songfinch sold one thing: an original song written for you by a real artist. Around $200, about a week to deliver. It converted under two percent. Most people who wanted a song could not accept the price or the wait.

The obvious answer in 2023 was to generate the songs. We did not, and I would make that call again.

Why generation was a non-starter
  • It kills the promise. The entire product is "a real artist wrote this for you." There is no version of that sentence that survives.
  • The artists walk. A supply network of hundreds of musicians was the actual asset. Announcing a generator burns it in a week.
  • Nobody could sign off on the rights. In 2023, and honestly still.
The reframe

The song did not need to be written on demand. It needed to be assembled on demand.

Which turned an unsolvable AI problem into a solvable operations problem. That reframe is most of the job.

Deep dive · 02The part that was mine

The hard problem was never the audio. It was making 300 musicians produce machine-readable output.

01 · The protocol

A recording spec artists would actually follow

Step-by-step instructions, session templates for Logic Pro X and Pro Tools with a track per required region, a test-track gate before anyone recorded the full set, and a review loop with named turnaround times. Musicians are not going to read a schema. They will follow a session template.

02 · The filename is the API

Structure encoded where it cannot get lost

10.1.1.1_V1.S1.L1_DATINGAPP

Bar.beat.division.tick, then song section, stanza and line, then the answer the stem satisfies. Every file self-describes its position and the customer answer it belongs to. Get this wrong and the system is manual forever, at any scale.

03 · The economics

A model artists opted into

$500 guaranteed on approved delivery, then $1 per song sold against recoupment. It made the ask legible: this is more work than a normal song, here is what it pays, here is the upside. Supply-side adoption is a compensation design problem, not a communication problem.

Deep dive · 02bWhere it stopped being a trick

Swapping a line is a trick. Saying your name is the moment.

We built a name dictionary using Synthesizer V, mapped every song's blueprint down to the syllable so we knew exactly which phrase we were replacing and how many syllables it had to absorb, and recorded vocal model templates capturing vowel, consonant, and performance data from each artist. The same line, tracked roughly sixteen times, to catch the range of cadence and phrasing.

The unglamorous middle

And the volume work under all of it

Batch processing across 2,000+ audio assets: FFmpeg pipelines for cleanup and versioning, naming enforcement, QA passes, delivery prep.

That is the unglamorous middle of every enablement job. If the batch layer is not automated, the clever layer above it never gets used, because someone is always three days behind on files.

Why the early ratio was trustworthy

Same site, same audience, same period, against an existing baseline with years of history behind it. The comparison was designed in before launch, so the launch produced a decision instead of a debate.

What I take from it

A clean comparison you set up in advance is worth more than a big number you have to explain afterwards. That is the whole argument for instrumenting first, and it is the thing I would bring here on day one.

Deep dive · 04The honest part

The assembly worked on day one. The instrumentation did not.

From our own post-launch review

  • 103 support tickets from customers who had already paid and hit a problem.
  • Previews would not load. Spinning wheels and error messages at the exact moment of highest intent.
  • 256 sold in Amplitude. 307 in Admin. We could not agree on how many songs we had sold.
  • Duplicate charges, stuck orders that never appeared in the system while the charge sat in Stripe.
  • The relationship categories did not fit real people. We shipped romantic first and everyone else bounced off the form.
The lesson I lead with now

The songs-sold discrepancy was the worst one, and it was not the audio.

A working product you cannot count is a product you cannot fund, prioritize, or defend. We spent the first weeks after a genuinely successful launch doing reconciliation instead of iteration, because the counting was built after the thing instead of into it.

What I would do differently

Instrument on day zero, and make the funnel legible before the launch, not after.

Every one of those failures was findable in a log on day one. Nobody was looking at a dashboard, because there wasn't one. That is the first thing I would build anywhere, including here.

ForwardWhat I would do here

You cannot enable what you cannot count.

Days 01 — 30 · Instrument

Find out what is actually happening.

Stand up usage reporting against the Claude Enterprise analytics API. Not seat counts. Which teams, which recurring jobs, which sessions start and never finish. Then sit with three teams and take the baselines by hand, because the API tells me what people did and only a person tells me what it cost them.

Days 31 — 60 · Ship one stem

One team, one job, one verifiable unit.

Pick the highest-frequency, most-checkable job in the instrumentation. Decompose it. Ship the stem into the surface that team already lives in. Three users first, then the team. A measured before-and-after, published whether it is good or not.

Days 61 — 90 · The dashboard

Make adoption a number leadership sees weekly.

Flow fingerprinting, cost per outcome, abandonment, and hours returned. The instrument that turns Scenario 3 from an archaeological dig into a Tuesday-afternoon glance.

Forward · contWhat the dashboard actually shows

Four numbers, and one honest caveat.

Metric 01

Flow fingerprinting

Cluster sessions by shape, not content. Answers the question nobody can answer today: what jobs are people actually doing with this?

Metric 02

Cost per outcome

Token spend per brief drafted, per proposal, per creator matched. Cost per seat is a procurement number. Cost per outcome is an enablement number.

Metric 03

Abandonment

Sessions that start and do not finish, ranked by frequency. This is the broken-stem list, and it writes my roadmap for me.

Metric 04

Hours returned

Baseline × volume. And I will say it on the slide: this is a modeled number, not a measured one.

The assumption ships visible on the chart. Enablement leads who hide that assumption get caught in the first finance review, and then every other number they present gets discounted too.

The workflow, as stems
  1. Brief in, structure out. The panel brief told me exactly what is being scored. That became the outline before a word was written.
  2. Source material parsed, not retyped. Four decks and two reports read and pulled apart programmatically.
  3. Argument first, pixels second. The scenario answers were drafted and attacked before any design existed.
  4. Assembly. One file, one render loop, one deploy.

Same shape as Instant Song. Decompose, make each piece verifiable on its own, assemble at the end. If I get this job that is the shape I will bring to every team that asks me for help.

Q&AThe brief says this is my chance to ask

Five things I want to know.

  1. When a team says an AI tool is not working, who hears it first today, and what happens next? I want to know if there is a path or if it dies in a Slack thread.
  2. What is the honest answer to "how many people at #paid used Claude last week"? If it is a guess, I know what I am building in month one.
  3. #paid publishes AI principles. Where has that actually constrained a build somebody wanted? Principles that have never cost anything are marketing. I would like to hear the time it cost something.
  1. Is this seat measured on adoption, on hours returned, or on workflows shipped? Those are three different jobs and I would rather know which one I am doing.
  2. What is the workflow everyone here has quietly given up on automating? That is usually the most interesting problem in the building, and it is usually a decomposition problem.
