IntroductionThe claim behind all four answers

Nobody at #paid is blocked by the model.

Every AI rollout I have seen fail, failed for one of three reasons, and none of them was capability.

01 · Context

The model has never seen your data.

#paid has authenticated creator audience data, 150+ creator categories, a benchmark panel of 30,000+ respondents, and a library of brand studies going back years. A general model has seen none of it, so it returns category-level marketing prose.

02 · Verification

They cannot tell if it came back right.

If checking the output costs more than doing the work, people quietly stop. Every time.

03 · Adoption

It lives somewhere they don't.

A tool outside the workflow is a tax, and the tax usually exceeds the saving.

Why this matters more here than most places

Your advantage is data that no model has seen.

Any competitor can buy the same model. What none of them have is which creator categories actually moved purchase intent for a QSR brand in-market, or which of your past campaigns is the right proof for the prospect on the phone right now.

That gap is the entire job. The work is not teaching people to prompt. It is connecting the model to the things you already know.

I will use this on all three scenarios. In Part 2 I will show you the build where I learned it, and the part of it that went wrong.

In order. Each one gets the same three beats: what I ask, what I do, what I measure. I have answered them for your business rather than in the abstract, so some of my assumptions will be wrong and I would rather you correct them.

Scenario 01Beat 1 · What I ask
The question

A VP says: "Claude isn't helping my team." How do you respond?

Looking for: discovery questions · diagnosing workflow problems · identifying measurable outcomes  ·  I am going to assume this is the VP of Sales.

I do not defend the tool and I do not offer training.

Both are the same mistake: treating a symptom as the problem. Training assumes the team is the broken part, and the team is almost never the broken part.

A VP saying this out loud is handing me a free diagnostic. Somebody on that team had a specific bad experience on a specific day, and the whole answer is downstream of finding out what it was.

So I stop talking about Claude and start asking about how a rep actually builds a pitch.

Scenario 01Beat 1 · The five questions I actually ask

Five questions, in this order.

  1. "Walk me through the last time a rep tried and it didn't work."
    A specific incident beats a general complaint. General complaints contain no failure point.
  2. "What did they expect to get back?"
    Nine times out of ten the ask was "write the pitch," which is the whole job, not a piece of it.
  3. "What does a rep do on the days they don't use it?"
    That is the real workflow, and it is the baseline I will measure against later.
  1. "Which part of a pitch would you not let a machine touch?"
    Finds the trust boundary. At #paid I would expect that to be anything a brand could hold you to.
  2. "If this worked perfectly, what would your reps stop doing?"
    The measurable outcome, in the VP's words and the VP's numbers. Not mine.
Question five is the one that matters. If we cannot finish that sentence together on the call, we are not ready to build anything, and I would rather say so than ship something neither of us can evaluate.

Scenario 01Beats 2 and 3 · What I do · What I measure

It is almost always one of four.

01

Wrong altitude

The ask was "write the pitch." Nobody can verify a whole pitch at a glance, so nobody trusts it.

02

No context

The model has never seen your brand studies, your creator categories, or your benchmark panel. It is guessing about the one thing you are actually selling.

03

No verdict

Output arrives with no way to tell good from bad, so checking costs more than doing.

04

Wrong seat

The rep holding the tool is not the person who owns the bottleneck. Sometimes it is the strategist.

What I do

Not the pitch. The evidence for the pitch.

Given a prospect's category, objective and market, return the three closest past campaigns with their real outcomes, plus the creator-category cut that supports the claim. Retrieval and assembly over your own data. Repetitive, high-frequency, and a rep can verify every line in ten seconds. It ships into the tool they already draft in, not a new one.

What I measure

Three numbers, agreed before I build.

Hours from qualified prospect to pitch-ready, pitches sent per rep per week, and win rate on pitches that carried a category-matched proof point against those that did not. That last one is the only one that survives a board meeting.

Scenario 02Business impact · Adoption · Strategic alignment · ROI
The question

Three requests, one sprint. Walk us through your prioritization. Sales wants proposal automation. Customer Success wants an onboarding assistant. Finance wants invoice categorization.

Scenario 02 · contBefore I rank anything

"Onboarding" means two completely different products here.

Brand side

Dozens of enterprise accounts, high touch.

A strategist is already in the room. The volume is low, the value per account is enormous, and the failure mode of a wrong answer is a damaged relationship with a household-name brand. This is the wrong place to put a v1.

Creator side

Thousands of creators, self-serve.

High volume, repetitive, and the same questions over and over. The failure mode is much cheaper and the frequency is far higher. This is a genuinely good candidate, and it is a different build with different economics.

I would not rank three requests when one of them is two requests. In a two-sided marketplace, "our customers" is always an ambiguous phrase, and the version of this I have seen go wrong is a team building for the loud side while the volume sits on the quiet one. Ten minutes of clarification changes the whole sprint.
Scenario 02 · contWhat the other two get

Sequencing is the skill. Ranking is not.

Nobody gets a no. A silent "later" is how this role loses the room in its first quarter.

Finance · this sprint

Invoice categorization ships.

Objectively checkable against a ledger, zero adoption resistance because nobody defends their invoice-coding turf, and one sprint to a number that survives a finance review. That number funds everything after it.

Sales · one day of this sprint

Instrumented, not built.

I spend a day measuring where pitch time actually goes, so that when Sales gets its sprint it is built against a baseline instead of a guess. The highest-value request deserves the best-prepared build.

Customer Success · next sprint, in writing

A date, a scope, and the reason.

Scoped to the creator side, where the volume is. High value and high adoption risk, and it needs the organizational trust that sprint one buys.

If the VP of Sales pulls rank

Real dynamic, and I do not fight it. There is a zero-cost version: the best existing pitch, the relevant case studies, and a shared prompt, out in an afternoon. It is not the build. It is discovery that happens to be useful, and how reps use it tells me what the real build should be.

What sprint one is actually buying

Not ROI. Evidence. Shipping the highest-value request first and producing something nobody can verify is the most common way this job stalls out before it starts.

Scenario 03The adoption cliff
The question

You built an AI workflow. People tried it once and no one uses it anymore. What do you do?

I will answer it with a concrete case: a tool that ranks incoming creator pitches for a campaign manager.

They tried it once. That is good news.

Rejection is never trying. One try means the pitch worked and the product didn't, so something specific happened on that first run. There is a failure point and it has a location.

Most likely, here

Checking cost more than doing

It ranked 200 creator pitches and showed a score. The manager could not see why number four beat number eleven, so they read all 200 anyway. The tool added a step.

Very likely

It broke on a real input

It worked on the demo campaign. A real one had a creator pitching in French, or a brief with three objectives. Nobody files a bug for an internal tool. They just stop.

Common

It lives in the wrong place

The work happens in the campaign workspace. If the ranking is in a separate tab, every switch is a tax and the tax exceeded the saving.

Quietly common

I saved the wrong 20 minutes

Ranking was the fun part to build. The slow part may have been writing the rejection notes, or checking audience authenticity.

Scenario 03 · contWhat I do · What I measure

Find the failure point, then rebuild smaller.

How I find out

  • Not a survey. People genuinely cannot tell you why they stopped. They will say they have been busy, and they will mean it.
  • The logs. Where did the last session end? That step is the bug, and it is usually one step.
  • Watch three campaign managers do it the old way. Forty-five minutes total, and I will see the workaround they built instead of telling me about it.

Then I rebuild smaller

  • Stop ranking. Just pull the one line from each pitch that answers the brief, and show it next to the creator's authenticated audience fit. The manager still decides, in a tenth of the time, and can see exactly what they are deciding on.
  • Inside the campaign workspace, not beside it.
  • Three people, not thirty. Adoption is earned per person before it is announced.
  • And I say the true sentence: I built the wrong thing, here is the smaller thing. That buys more credibility than a working v1 does.
The real fix is upstream: this should never have been an archaeological dig. Instrumented on day one, "tried once and stopped" is a number on a dashboard the week it happens instead of a mystery I solve two months later. That is the thread I want to pick back up at the end.
Part 2 of 2 · 20 minutes · Songfinch, 2022—2025

Instant Song.

We took a product that took a week and made it take seconds, without generating a single note. I picked this story because the constraint we worked under is the one you published.

Photo · cottonbro studio
Deep dive · 00Why I chose it for this room

"Help, not do" was our engineering constraint before it was your principle.

Your first published AI principle is that AI should amplify creators rather than replace them, and that you will not touch unlicensed voice or likeness for commercial gain.

In 2023 I was on a team that had every commercial reason to generate songs and did not, for exactly those reasons. Everything that follows is what it costs to hold that line and still ship something that works, which I suspect is a conversation you have here regularly.

The same problem, both companies

A marketplace only works if the supply side trusts you.

Songfinch's asset was hundreds of musicians. Yours is creators. In both cases the supply side can leave, and they talk to each other.

Every technical decision in this story was constrained by whether the artists would say yes to it. That is not a values slide, it is an engineering requirement, and it changed what we were allowed to build.

Deep dive · 01The problem

The funnel had a wall, and the obvious fix was the wrong one.

Songfinch sold one thing: an original song written for you by a real artist. Around $200, about a week to deliver. It converted under two percent. Most people who wanted a song could not accept the price or the wait.

The obvious answer in 2023 was to generate the songs. We did not, and I would make that call the same way again.

Why generation was a non-starter
  • It kills the promise. The entire product is "a real artist wrote this for you." No version of that sentence survives.
  • The artists walk. A supply network of hundreds of musicians was the actual asset. Announcing a generator burns it in a week.
  • Nobody could sign off on the rights. In 2023, and honestly still.
The reframe

The song did not need to be written on demand. It needed to be assembled on demand.

That turned an unsolvable AI problem into a solvable operations problem. The reframe is most of the job.

Deep dive · 02How it works · click any answer

Four questions. Seconds. Nothing generated.

Assembled output
Static · recorded once Stem · selected at runtime

The catalogue is finite and a person sang every piece of it. Nothing is created at request time, which is exactly why it is fast, cheap, on-brand, and legally clean.

Deep dive · 03My part of it

The hard problem was never the audio. It was getting 300 musicians to produce machine-readable output.

01 · The protocol

A recording spec artists would actually follow

Step-by-step instructions, session templates for Logic Pro X and Pro Tools with a track per required region, a test-track gate before anyone recorded a full set, and a review loop with named turnaround times. Musicians will not read a schema. They will follow a session template.

02 · The filename is the API

Structure encoded where it cannot get lost

10.1.1.1_V1.S1.L1_DATINGAPP

Bar.beat.division.tick, then song section, stanza and line, then the answer the stem satisfies. Every file self-describes its position and which customer answer it belongs to. Get this wrong and the system is manual forever, at any scale.

03 · The economics

A model artists opted into

A guarantee on approved delivery, then a per-song royalty against recoupment. It made the ask legible: this is more work than a normal song, here is what it pays, here is the upside if it works. Supply-side adoption is a compensation design problem, not a communication problem.

Under all of it, the unglamorous middle: batch processing across 2,000+ audio assets with FFmpeg pipelines for cleanup and versioning, naming enforcement, QA passes and delivery prep. If the batch layer is not automated, the clever layer above it never gets used, because somebody is always three days behind on files.
Deep dive · 04The hardest conversation

Swapping a line is a trick. Saying your name is the moment.

Customers reacted to hearing the name, not to the personalized lyric. So we built a name dictionary using Synthesizer V, mapped each song's blueprint down to the syllable so we knew exactly which phrase was being replaced and how many syllables it had to absorb, and captured vocal model templates carrying each artist's vowel, consonant and performance data.

The same line, tracked roughly sixteen times, to cover the range of cadence a name has to survive.

This is the part you should ask me about

We were asking musicians for vocal data in the worst possible year to ask.

If that conversation goes badly there is no product, no matter how good the pipeline is. What worked was being specific: exactly what we captured, exactly what it would and would not be used for, scoped to their own songs, with pay attached.

Adoption is a trust problem wearing a technical costume. Your creators will be asked a version of this question, and the answer has to be concrete before anyone builds anything.

Deep dive · 07The honest part

The assembly worked on day one. The instrumentation did not.

From our own post-launch review

  • 103 support tickets from customers who had already paid and then hit a problem.
  • Previews would not load. Spinning wheels and error messages at the exact moment of highest intent.
  • Two systems disagreed on how many songs we had sold. Analytics said one number, admin said another.
  • Duplicate charges and stuck orders that never appeared in the system while the charge sat in the payment processor.
  • The relationship categories did not fit real people. We shipped romantic first and everyone else bounced off the form.
The one that actually mattered

A working product you cannot count is a product you cannot fund, prioritize, or defend.

The sales discrepancy was worse than any audio problem. We spent the weeks after a genuinely successful launch doing reconciliation instead of iteration, because the counting was built after the thing instead of into it.

What I would do differently

Instrument on day zero.

Every one of those failures was findable in a log on day one. Nobody was looking at a dashboard, because there wasn't one.

Deep dive · 08What I would do here

You cannot enable what you cannot count.

Scenario 3 and the thing that went wrong at Songfinch are the same problem. Here is how I would make sure neither happens here.

Days 01 — 30 · Instrument

Find out what is actually happening.

Usage reporting against the Claude Enterprise analytics API. Not seat counts: which teams, which recurring jobs, which sessions start and never finish. Then sit with sales, a strategist, and a campaign manager and take baselines by hand, because the API tells me what people did and only a person tells me what it cost them.

Days 31 — 60 · Wire up the context

One job, connected to your own data.

On current evidence I would start with pitch evidence for sales, because it is high-frequency, instantly checkable, and it is the clearest case of the model being blind to something you already own. Three reps first, then the team, with a measured before and after published either way.

Days 61 — 90 · Make it visible

Adoption becomes a number leadership sees weekly.

Which flows get used, cost per outcome rather than per seat, where sessions get abandoned, and hours returned. Hours returned is a modeled number and the assumption ships visible on the chart, because an enablement lead who hides that gets caught in the first finance review.

So the most relevant thing about this deck is how it was made, not how it looks.

One HTML file. Three.js and GSAP served from the deck itself, so it does not need the internet in this room. Built with Claude Code, deployed to Vercel.

The workflow
  1. Brief in, structure out. Your brief says exactly what is being scored, so it became the outline before a word was written.
  2. Context first. Your site, your case studies, your AI principles, read before any answer was drafted. Same move I am proposing for your reps.
  3. Argument before pixels. The scenario answers were written and attacked before any design existed.
  4. Assembly. One file, one deploy.
Panel Q&AThe brief says this is also my chance to ask

Five things I want to know.

  1. How much of your first-party data is actually reachable by a model today? Brand studies, creator categories, benchmark panel. If the answer is "none of it," that is my first quarter and I would like to know now.
  2. When a team says an AI tool is not working, who hears it first, and what happens next? I want to know whether there is a path or whether it dies in a Slack thread.
  3. Which side of the marketplace is under more operational strain right now? Brand or creator. The answer changes what I build first.
  1. Where have your AI principles actually cost you something? Principles that have never blocked a build are marketing. I would like to hear about the time one did.
  2. Is this seat measured on adoption, hours returned, or workflows shipped? Those are three different jobs and I would rather know which one I am doing.
